# Australian Privacy — a plain-English starter note

> **This is general awareness, not legal advice.** It is a starting point to help a small business
> *think* about privacy — not a compliance program and not a determination of what the law requires of
> you. Whether (and how) the Privacy Act and the Australian Privacy Principles apply to your business
> depends on your circumstances. For anything that matters, check the OAIC's guidance and get advice.

## Why this note exists

If your business holds information about identifiable people — clients, customers, leads, contractors —
you are handling **personal information**. Treating that information carefully is both the right thing and,
for many businesses, a legal expectation. This note helps you build good habits early, so privacy isn't a
fire drill later.

## Does the Privacy Act apply to me?

It depends. As a general guide only:

- The **Privacy Act 1988 (Cth)** and the **Australian Privacy Principles (APPs)** apply to most
  Australian Government agencies and to many private-sector organisations.
- Many **small businesses** (broadly, those under a turnover threshold) have historically been exempt —
  **but there are important exceptions**, and the law in this area changes. Common situations where a
  small business *is* covered include businesses that trade in personal information, that provide health
  services and hold health information, or that are contracted service providers to government.
- **Health information** is "sensitive information" and carries higher obligations; a business that holds
  it is often covered regardless of size.
- Even where the Act doesn't strictly apply, customers increasingly expect good privacy practice, and
  other rules (such as the Spam Act for marketing) may still apply.

**Do not assume you're exempt.** Confirm your position using the OAIC's "does my small business need to
comply" guidance, and revisit it when your business changes (new services, government contracts, handling
health data).

## The Australian Privacy Principles, in plain terms

The 13 APPs are the backbone of Australian privacy practice. Here they are as plain habits:

1. **Be open about how you handle information.** Have a privacy stance you can explain.
2. **Let people deal with you anonymously where practical.** Don't collect identity you don't need.
3. **Only collect what you actually need**, and collect it fairly and lawfully.
4. **Handle unsolicited information carefully** — if you didn't need to collect it, consider destroying it.
5. **Tell people when you collect their information**, and why.
6. **Use it only for the purpose you collected it for** (or a closely related one they'd expect).
7. **Don't use personal information for direct marketing** unless you're allowed to and people can opt out.
8. **Be careful sending information overseas** — including to overseas cloud providers.
9. **Handle government identifiers carefully** (e.g. don't adopt a TFN as your own customer ID).
10. **Keep information accurate and up to date.**
11. **Keep it secure** — protect it from misuse, loss, and unauthorised access.
12. **Give people access** to their own information when they ask.
13. **Correct it** when it's wrong.

You don't need to be a lawyer to act on these. Most are common sense made explicit.

## Seven practical habits for a solo / small operator

1. **Collect less.** The information you never collected can't be breached, lost, or mishandled.
2. **Say why.** When you ask for details, tell people what you'll use them for. A line on your form or
   site is often enough to start.
3. **Keep it secure.** Encrypt your devices and backup drives; use strong, unique passwords and a
   password manager; turn on multi-factor authentication. See `SOP-data-backup-3-2-1`.
4. **Know where it lives.** Local-first by default (see `ADR-0001`); know which cloud tools hold copies,
   and whether they store data overseas.
5. **Don't keep it forever.** Destroy or de-identify personal information you no longer need and aren't
   required to keep. See `SOP-record-retention-AU`.
6. **Have a breach plan.** If information is lost or exposed, you'll respond far better with a checklist
   than from panic. See `SOP-incident-record`. Be aware the **Notifiable Data Breaches** scheme requires
   some organisations to notify affected people and the OAIC about eligible breaches — check whether it
   applies to you.
7. **Honour requests.** If someone asks what you hold about them, or asks you to correct it, take it
   seriously and respond.

## Marketing: a quick flag

If you send marketing emails or messages, the **Spam Act 2003 (Cth)** generally requires consent, accurate
sender identification, and a working unsubscribe — even for businesses otherwise exempt from the Privacy
Act. Keep your consent records (see your retention SOP).

## Where to check (authoritative sources)

- **Office of the Australian Information Commissioner (OAIC)** — oaic.gov.au — the regulator; has small-business guidance, the APP guidelines, and the Notifiable Data Breaches scheme.
- **Australian Cyber Security Centre (ACSC)** — cyber.gov.au — practical security guidance for small business.
- A **lawyer or privacy adviser** for your specific situation.
